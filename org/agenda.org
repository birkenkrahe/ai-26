** Entry Survey Summary — What This Class Looks Like (Week 1)

*** Background & Experience
- The class has a *very mixed technical background*.
- Several students report *no prior programming experience*.
- Many have *light exposure* (Excel, one CS course, scripting, or
  tutorials).
- A small number have stronger technical backgrounds, but they are *not
  the majority*.

*Takeaway:* This course cannot assume shared technical foundations — and
it does not need to.

*** Prior Use of Generative AI
- Almost everyone has already used *ChatGPT* or a similar tool.
- Typical uses include:
  - Writing assistance
  - Homework support
  - Brainstorming ideas
  - Summarization
- Very few students have used AI for:
  - Automation
  - Multi-step workflows
  - File-based or repeatable tasks

*Takeaway:* Students are experienced *users* of AI, but not yet *designers*
of AI workflows.

*** Confidence vs. Understanding
- Many students feel AI is *useful*.
- Far fewer feel confident explaining:
  - *How* AI arrives at an answer
  - *Why* it sometimes fails
  - *When* its output should not be trusted

*Takeaway:* There is a gap between *getting answers* and *understanding
processes*.

*** Motivation for Taking the Course
- Common motivations include:
  - Wanting practical, real-world skills
  - Curiosity about AI beyond hype
  - Improving productivity and efficiency
  - Preparing for future jobs or graduate study
- Very few students cited theory or math as a primary motivation.

*Takeaway:* This group values *usefulness*, *transferable skills*, and
*hands-on work*.

*** What This Means for the Course
- We will:
  - Start from *conversation and reasoning*, not code
  - Treat AI as a *collaborator*, not an oracle
  - Focus on *workflows*, not one-off prompts
  - Expect everyone to explain what their AI system is doing
- Prior programming experience is *not required* to succeed here.

*Bottom line:* This course is about learning how to *work with* AI
responsibly and deliberately — not just how to get answers from it.

** Discussion (Thu)

The DataCamp video ("What can AI do?") from chapter 1 states:
#+begin_quote
»AI is only as good as the data it learns from.«
#+end_quote

In IT, this is also called "Garbage in, garbage out." (GIGO)

Discuss:
1) Do you think this is true?
   #+begin_quote
   Yes, but it's incomplete: Bad data sets the limits of what an AI
   can reliably learn; good data does not guarantee good behavior.
   #+end_quote
2) Can you find an example for 'bad data' leading to 'bad AI'?
   #+begin_quote
   - Facial recognition systems respond directly to training data
     based on skin color - e.g. cannot see X-skinned people.
   - Medical AI trained on data from one population failing on
     another. Skin cancer recognition depending on skin color.
   - Hiring systems encode discrimination when based on certain data.
   #+end_quote
3) Is this true for humans?
   #+begin_quote
   Yes, and no. Humans also learn from examples, experiences,
   feedback. "Bad data" correspond to: lack of information, biased
   feedback. But humans can reflect on their data and question
   it. They can also use bad data to develop resilience to it
   (e.g. click bait, email scams, fake news).
   #+end_quote
4) Can you find a counterexample ('bad data' leading to 'good AI')?
5) Can you find a counterexample for human decisions?

#+begin_notes
- 'Bad' data are: noisy, missing, or incorrect.
- 'Good AI' is AI that makes predictions with high accuracy.
- Example for 4): AI "toxicity training" ([[https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.04741][Li et al, 2025]])
- Examples for 5): Scientific serendipity (failed experiments leading
  to good scientific results): Discovery of penicillin by accident
  through Fleming in 1928; Vicarious learning or "crash test" effect:
  humans can learn from other people's bad data without experiencing
  it themselves.
#+end_notes

** Post-mortem: The 10 Commandments of AI-Collaborative Coding
#+attr_html: :width 400px :float nil:
[[../img/ten_commandments_info.png]]

Here are a few extra tips for building code (as in web apps) with AI,
since it's not just about the *product*, but also (or more) about the
*process*:

1) *Structure your prompts* (goal, input, output, mechanics, behavior,
   buttons, etc.)
2) *Be specific.* Write short sentences. Make it easy for the machine to
   get you.
3) Ask for the *prompt* to be added as a comment at the top of the file.
4) Ask for a *program header* with title, author (you + AI), license,
   input, output, etc.
5) Look at your prompts as if they were programming *functions*:
   tag/name them (promptID).
6) Don't ask AI to fix the whole app once it gets large - *refactor*
   only parts of it.
7) Define a *single source of truth* (state management): for example,
   "track all variables in one object named =gameState="
8) Use chain of *verification:* before asking for the final code, ask AI
   to list the logic then write the code (reduces hallucinations)
9) Specify *edge case* handling: ask the AI to use semantic (meaningful)
   names.
10) Always ask the AI to *explain why* it changed something.

1. What does "2nd sprint" mean to you?
   #+begin_quote
   - A sprint is a period of 2-4 weeks in an *agile Scrum* project.
   - During the sprint, teams work on the backlog of all the things
     they need to do to build a successful *prototype* for shipping.
   - In the sprint *review* at the end of each sprint, the team presents
     their prototype to the client and the other teams for review.
   - The main purpose of the review is to solicit comments and get
     input for the next sprint.
   #+end_quote

2. Has your project confidence index changed?
   #+begin_quote
   - If it went up from where it was 3 weeks ago, you can relax.
   - If it went down from where it was 3 weeks ago, you have to make
   changes so that you bring it back to at least a 5 (50%) or better.
   #+end_quote

3. What counts as a good source?
   #+begin_quote
   1. *Recent* (depending on quality/field) but not too recent (unchecked)
   2. *Citations* (other publications referring to the source)
   3. Authors' *reputation* (indirectly or directly)
   4. *Style* and *tone* of the publication (journalistic/commercial)
   5. *Place of publication* (science, commerce, independent)
   6. *Relevance* for your topic (you can *use* it)
   7. Realistically *doable* (you can *understand* it)
   8. *Structure* of the publication (you can summarize it)
   9. *Evidence* (references inside the source)
   10. *Purpose* (*bias* of the source)
   #+end_quote
4. How can you evaluate a source?
   #+begin_quote
   1. *Identify* the author and place of publication.
   2. Check *when* it was published or last updated.
   3. Look for evidence: data, *references*, or links to other work.
   4. *Compare* the claims with at least one or two other sources.
   5. Ask what the *purpose* of the source is (inform, argue, sell,
      persuade).
   6. Check whether the claims are specific and supported, or vague
      and opinion-based.
   7. Decide whether the source actually helps answer your research
      question.
   8. Read the first sentence
   9. Check if authors have titles (expertise)
   10. Check if the title is meaningful
   11. Check if there are conclusions and if they are honest.
   #+end_quote
5. How can you move from your proposal to a step-by-step plan?
   #+begin_quote
   1. *Restate* your project goal in one clear sentence.
   2. Identify the main *inputs* (information, tools, or data you need).
   3. Break the task into major stages (research, design, testing,
      evaluation).
   4. Turn each stage into *concrete actions* you can perform.
   5. Put the actions in a *logical* order.
   6. Add simple *checks* to verify whether each step worked.
   7. Write the plan so *another* person could follow it without
      guessing.

** Key trends 2026 ([[file:~/GitHub/admin/spring26/dsc482/pdf/AI_Trends_2026.pdf][slide deck]])
#+attr_html: :width 400px :float nil:
[[../img/trends.png]]

*Five AI Trends Shaping 2026*

Source: [[https://youtu.be/cqLALf4bFPI?si=lKsaWZqCSTuRN7CZ][ByteByteGo, 11 March 2026 (YouTube)]]

*1. Reasoning Models*
- Early chatbots responded immediately. They generated text token
  by token.
- This worked for writing but failed on multi-step tasks like math
  or coding.
- Reasoning models pause and compute before answering.
- They break problems into smaller steps.
- Training is shifting from *Reinforcement Learning from Human
  Feedback (RLHF)*.
- Toward *Reinforcement Learning from Verifiable Rewards (RLVR)*.
- RLVR uses automatic checks (tests, correct answers) instead of
  human ratings.
- Models also adjust reasoning effort depending on task complexity.

*2. Agents*
- Early models answered questions only.
- New models can *use tools*.
- Agents decide when to call tools and how to use them.
- Current agents handle short tasks well.
- Long workflows still fail due to context loss and accumulated
  errors.
- Many agents run in cloud sandboxes with limited system access.
- The next step is *persistent local agents* running for hours or
  days.

*3. Coding AI*
- Early tools provided simple autocomplete.
- They only saw a few lines around the cursor.
- New coding models understand entire repositories.
- They are trained on real codebases and documentation.
- They run in loops using tools: read files, search and edit code,
  and run tests.
- Open-source coding models are improving quickly.
- Some can run on personal machines.

*4. Open-Weight Models*
- Top models used to be closed behind APIs.
- Users could call them but not run them locally.
- Open-weight releases changed this.
- Developers can now run and fine-tune models.
- The focus is shifting to accessibility.
- New architectures improve efficiency.
- Self-hosting models is becoming practical.

*5. Multimodal AI*
- Early models processed text only.
- Modern models handle images, video, audio, and text.
- They can reason across modalities in one interaction.
- Image and video generation quality has improved significantly.
- Two emerging directions:
  1) *Physical AI* (robots in real environments)
  2) *World models* (AI simulating physics and environments)

*Overall Direction*: AI systems are becoming:
- better at reasoning
- capable of acting through tools
- able to generate and maintain software
- accessible through open models
- integrated across multiple modalities

** DONE 10 Review Questions (Machine Learning Session)

1. What is the key difference between traditional programming and
   machine learning?
   #+begin_quote
   Traditional programming uses data + rules → output, while machine
   learning uses data + known outputs → learns rules (a model).
   #+end_quote

2. What does it mean that machine learning is “not explicitly
   programmed”?
   #+begin_quote
   The system learns behavior from data instead of following
   hand-coded rules.
   #+end_quote

   #+begin_quote
   Data provides the input and, in supervised learning, the labeled
   outputs used to train models.
   #+end_quote

   #+begin_quote
   A model is a set of learned rules derived from data and previous
   outputs.
   #+end_quote

5. What is supervised learning?
   #+begin_quote
   Learning from labeled data where both inputs and correct outputs
   are known.
   #+end_quote

6. What is unsupervised learning?
   #+begin_quote
   Learning patterns or structure (e.g., clusters) from data without
   labeled outputs.
   #+end_quote

7. What is reinforcement learning?
   #+begin_quote
   Learning through feedback (reward or penalty) based on the
   correctness of outputs.
   #+end_quote

8. Why is machine learning described as an optimization problem?
   #+begin_quote
   Because it minimizes a measurable error between predictions and
   actual outcomes.
   #+end_quote

9. What is an example of a supervised learning task discussed in
